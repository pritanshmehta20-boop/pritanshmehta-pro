5 Questions Every Media-Literate Person Should Ask

A simple framework for thinking more critically about the media messages we consume and produce.

By Pritansh Mehta September 2026

Media Literacy refers to the ability to critically analyze and ethically disseminate media to the public and masses. It is highly dependent on how you consume and produce media in a way that contributes to the overall wellbeing of the society.

Its roots can be traced back to 1920-1930s British Film Institute's campaign to teach students analytical skills about media consumption. The term “media literacy” is widely credited to John Culkin, a Jesuit priest, educator, and media scholar who worked in the 1960s to help teachers and students critically understand how media shapes culture and learning. Media literacy helps an individual to skillfully discern between what is useful and what is not from the vast pool of messages communicated by a particular form of media such that it helps. It aids an individual in identifying misinformation and promote right media consumption strongly.

Here are 5 questions that each media literate should ask:

1. Who created this message?

It is extremely important to identify the source of the message i.e. from where the information has come out. Is it from a person, group or institution? It is important to look at the account that communicated the message as it helps in building trust and authenticity. This is one of the fundamental steps in becoming a media literate.

2. What is the purpose of this message?

Next is to identify the purpose behind the communicated message. Is it to inform, educate or entertain? Is it meant to hurt the sentiments or establish a connection among two parties? Is it useful or meant to evoke a particular emotional reaction? Is it meant to persuade or sell anything or is genuinely helpful?

3. What evidence supports the claim?

Identify the evidence behind the piece of information so that it is not just a baseless claim but is based on some concrete truth. Look for sources, data, documents, original reporting, and whether the evidence actually supports the claim.

4. What might be missing or left out?

To assume that the piece of information is complete and wholistic is a naive presupposition. Ask what perspectives, facts, context, or counterarguments are absent from the presentation. That will help in understanding the complete picture.

5. How is the message trying to influence me?

Examining the use of language, images, headlines, emotions, fear, outrage, authority, repetition, or other persuasive techniques is extremely important in order to understand how the message is being used and what is the purpose it is trying to achieve. Once that is clear, it is much easier to categorize the intention behind every message and what it demands from you.
